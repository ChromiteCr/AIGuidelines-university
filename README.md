## Cross-school rules worth adapting for high school

- **Instructor/course rules come first:** Students must check whether AI is allowed for each class and assignment; ask before use when uncertain.
- **AI is assistance, not authorship:** Do not submit AI-generated work as your own or let AI replace the thinking and skill practice the assignment is designed to assess.
- **Disclose and document:** Identify tools, prompts, relevant outputs, and how AI was incorporated; retain drafts and chat records where appropriate.
- **Verify everything:** Students remain responsible for accuracy, fabricated citations, bias, appropriateness, and copyright issues.
- **Protect privacy and confidential information:** Do not upload personal data, credentials, classmates’ work, assessments, unpublished research, or restricted course materials to public AI systems.
- **Respect intellectual property:** Do not upload or redistribute copyrighted or instructor-owned materials without permission.
- **Use approved tools and age-appropriate accounts:** Schools should specify approved platforms and account/consent rules, especially for students under 18.
- **Consequences follow existing academic-integrity systems:** Undisclosed or prohibited AI use should be handled consistently with plagiarism and unauthorized-assistance rules.

## Additional lessons from universities 6–10

- **A high-school-facing model already exists:** Penn’s Wharton Global Youth policy permits brainstorming but prohibits submitting AI output as one’s own and requires disclosure in Works Cited.
- **Default rule when unclear:** Columbia and Duke treat unapproved generative-AI assistance as unauthorized; students should ask the teacher before using it.
- **Rules may vary by assignment:** Northwestern explicitly makes students responsible for checking each instructor’s directions, even within the same course.
- **Attribution does not automatically make every use acceptable:** UChicago’s policy distinguishes permitted assistance from outsourcing authorship; citing AI does not cure a use that the assignment prohibits.
- **AI detectors should not be sole evidence:** Columbia and Duke warn about false positives and recommend human review and conversation.
- **Sensitive data need explicit protection:** UChicago prohibits entering confidential or protected information into public AI systems without appropriate review.

## Additional lessons from universities 11–15

- **Treat public AI input as potential public disclosure:** Princeton and Caltech stress the use of approved tools for non-public institutional information; Caltech says uploads should be considered public disclosure unless confidentiality is explicitly guaranteed.
- **Use assignment-level permission labels:** Johns Hopkins’ red/yellow/green framework and Brown’s five-level model make clear that acceptable use may range from fully prohibited to deliberately integrated.
- **Permission never removes accountability:** Johns Hopkins, Caltech, Brown, and Vanderbilt all retain expectations for transparency, verification, and human responsibility.
- **Disclose the tool, purpose, and human contribution:** Princeton and Brown offer practical models for a short AI-use statement and, where useful, prompt logs or original drafts.
- **Do not rely on detector scores as proof:** Vanderbilt states that an Undergraduate Honor Council report cannot rest solely on an AI-detector score and highlights privacy risks and bias against non-native English writers.
- **Frame AI as augmentation:** Vanderbilt recommends using AI to amplify human creativity and reasoning rather than substitute for them.

## Additional lessons from universities 16–25

- **Default rules differ, so clarity matters:** Dartmouth and Notre Dame prohibit unapproved use by default; Rice lets course-specific rules supersede its general policy; CMU emphasizes defining authorized assistance explicitly.
- **Use graduated permission levels:** UNC’s four levels distinguish no use, assistive use, partial generation, and full generation. This is especially adaptable to high-school assignments.
- **Protect both student and teacher data:** Berkeley, UCLA, Georgetown, and Emory prohibit or restrict uploading student records, unpublished work, university data, intellectual property, and instructor-created materials to unapproved systems.
- **Document the process, not only the final answer:** Berkeley, Rice, Dartmouth, CMU, and Emory suggest acknowledgments, prompts, outputs, drafts, or editing history as evidence of transparent learning.
- **Detector scores require caution:** Berkeley does not recommend AI detectors; Rice will not treat a detector as sole or primary evidence; Michigan highlights reliability and false-positive concerns.
- **AI output is not research evidence:** Michigan stresses that GenAI is not a search engine; UCLA, CMU, Notre Dame, and UNC require human review for accuracy, bias, fabricated claims, and source problems.
- **Student responsibility remains central:** Georgetown, UNC, Notre Dame, and Rice keep the human learner accountable for the final product, even when AI is permitted.
- **Rules should preserve genuine learning:** Notre Dame distinguishes supplementing education from replacing intellectual engagement; Rice and UNC similarly state that AI should support rather than replace thinking.

## Additional lessons from universities 26–30

- **Institutional access is not course permission:** USC’s ChatGPT Edu access does not override instructor rules; students at WashU, UVA, UC San Diego, and NYU must likewise check the applicable course or assignment rule.
- **Disclose the process, including non-writing help:** WashU and UVA extend transparency beyond copied prose to uses such as brainstorming, editing, translation, prompts, and fact-checking.
- **Verify the underlying evidence:** NYU directs students to open AI-supplied links and corroborate important claims; all five schools keep the human user responsible for accuracy and invented citations.
- **Protect student work and identity:** UVA highlights students’ intellectual property and the need for an alternative to compulsory uploads; NYU Steinhardt warns against placing student identifiers in unlicensed systems.
- **Approved platforms reduce—but do not erase—risk:** UC San Diego and USC provide institutionally supported tools, yet users still must follow course rules, data classifications, privacy duties, and copyright limits.
- **Detector output is not proof:** UVA, USC, and UC San Diego explicitly question detector reliability; fair review should emphasize drafts, source records, context, and conversation. The reviewed NYU-domain pages do not establish a university-wide detector-evidence rule.

## Policy Atlas — 政策图谱

A dynamic comparison of all thirty policies across 12 comparable provisions
(360 cells). Open `atlas/index.html` in a browser.

**每所学校独立抽取，互不串源。** 30 份文件由 30 个互相隔离的 agent 分别读取，每个只被允许
读它自己那一份，并被禁止调用任何关于该校的既有知识；随后 30 个对抗性审计 agent 重读同一份
文件，专门找编造的引文、把「建议」写成「强制」的抬级、以及本该沉默却被填上的格子（本轮共
修正 68 处）。最后所有引文与其所属文件做精确字符串比对——**234/234 条逐字命中，零串源**。

### Pipeline

```
univ/*.md
   └─ 隔离抽取 + 对抗审计（工作流）
        └─ tools/build_data.py   校验引文 / 归一取值 / 计算分布  →  data/policies.json
             └─ tools/build_atlas.py   注入数据到模板            →  atlas/index.html
```

图谱不硬编码任何一条规则，全部读 `data/policies.json`；条款口径统一定义在
`tools/codebook.py` 一处。改源文件、重跑构建，图谱自动更新。

```bash
python3 tools/build_data.py <raw-extraction.json> && python3 tools/build_atlas.py
```

`tools/from_journal.py` 可从工作流的 transcript 目录重建抽取结果，用于断点续跑或复核。

### 12 项条款

| 分组 | 条款 |
|------|------|
| 许可 | 默认规则、课程规则优先、分级许可 |
| 透明 | 披露要求、过程留证 |
| 责任 | 核实责任、违规定性、检测器证据 |
| 边界 | 数据与隐私、知识产权、机构工具 |
| 理由 | 学习理由 |

### 本轮读数

- **可以照抄的**（过半学校有规定且口径最一致）：数据与隐私 72%、课程规则优先 70%、学习理由 60%
- **得自己判断的**（同样过半有规定但分歧最大）：核实责任 39%、披露要求 43%、默认规则 45%
- **大学集体没说的**（沉默率最高）：过程留证 20/30、检测器证据 19/30、机构工具 19/30

360 格中 234 格查有明文，126 格该来源沉默。沉默不等于该校无规定，只表示本项目采集的这份
官方来源对此未着一字——而这些空白正是高中场景里最需要自己补上的部分。
