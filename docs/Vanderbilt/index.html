[S1-Q1] AI as a source of assistance, with brainstorming/grammar/feedback analogies

We understand that many students will use various forms of assistance as they prepare their application; ChatGPT and other forms of AI may be viewed as one of these sources of assistance. For example, students may rely on an essay-writing class to help brainstorm topic ideas for their essay. Or they may use grammar tools available online to check grammar in their writing. Or students may ask a parent or guardian or peer to read their essay and offer feedback on clarity or offer advice regarding structure of the essay.

[S1-Q2] No replacement of independent thought or rewriting for the applicant

However, students should understand that it would not be proper to ask a teacher or parent to come up with the essay topic or to re-write an essay for the student. Likewise, AI should never be used to replace independent thinking on the part of the applicant.

[S1-Q3] Own voice and own experience

As applicants complete the essay portions of the application, they should always use their own voice and write about their own life experiences.

What is the university's policy on the use of generative AI?

The university empowers instructors to establish their own policies on the use of generative AI in the classroom, within the guidelines of the Honor Code.课程规则优先Instructor override It is the instructor’s responsibility to determine how or if students may use these tools and to communicate expectations clearly.

If an instructor does not provide a statement on the use of generative AI tools, then the university permits students to use generative AI tools, but they must disclose all generative AI usage.默认规则Default rule披露要求Disclosure

Guidance asks instructors to make clear:

  • whether generative AI is allowed;
  • whether it may be used for brainstorming, proofreading, or composing text;分级许可Tiered scale
  • what constitutes appropriate, ethical use and academic misconduct;
  • what confidentiality and privacy rules apply;数据与隐私Data & privacy
  • how students must disclose and/or cite AI use;
  • that students remain responsible for AI-generated content; and
  • whether expectations differ across assignments.

Detection and fair process

There are currently no foolproof methods for detecting the use of generative AI. Vanderbilt notes that traditional plagiarism checkers are not reliable sources of generative AI detection and that publicly available AI-detection tools may compromise student privacy protections.

The university highlights several limitations:

  • unknown privacy and data-usage policies may put FERPA-protected information at risk;
  • tools often lack public, detailed methodologies and standards;
  • early studies suggest bias against non-native English speakers.

Current guidelines indicate that a report to the Undergraduate Honor Council cannot be based solely on an artificial intelligence detector score.检测器证据Detector evidence When examining a suspected violation, relevant factors may include assignment instructions, syllabus policies, comparisons with the student’s work in that course, instructor analysis, and student and instructor testimony.违规定性Integrity framing AI-detection scores generally are not considered.

High-school adaptation takeaways

  • Teachers should state the AI rule clearly for every course and any assignment that differs from the default.
  • If a school adopts a default-allowed rule, all AI use should still be disclosed; alternatively, a high school may choose a stricter default because minors need clearer boundaries.
  • Do not treat an AI-detector score as proof of misconduct.
  • Investigations should consider assignment instructions, drafts, the student’s course work, and a conversation with the student.
  • AI should augment—not substitute for—student reasoning, and students must check outputs for errors.
