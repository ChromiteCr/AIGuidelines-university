## Honor Council AI Policy and Information

- Publishing unit: Rice Honor Council
- Official source: https://honor.rice.edu/ai-policy-information

---

Rice’s general policy states:

> “Utilizing AI software to generate ideas and pass them off as one’s own will also be considered plagiarism and will be adjudicated as such.”

The policy governs academic work, but professors may create course-specific AI policies, and “any course-specific AI policy supersedes the Honor Council’s general AI policy.”

## Detector evidence

The Honor Council permits detector results to support the opening of an investigation. However, “due to their inaccuracy, the Council will not use detector software as the sole or primary evidence in an adjudication”; results may serve only as supplementary evidence.

The Council suggests using document editing history to help establish student-produced work and designing prompts that require engagement with course material and critical thinking.

## Student-facing responsible-use guidance

- Additional official source: https://responsibleai.rice.edu/guidelines-resources-students

The student guidance says AI tools are allowed only when the instructor explicitly permits them. Each course may differ, so students should check the syllabus or ask before using AI.

Submitting AI-generated work as one’s own may be academic dishonesty, including AI-generated essays, code, data analysis, or ideas. The guidance frames responsible AI as a collaboration tool, not a replacement for thinking.

## High-school adaptation takeaways

- Establish a default rule but allow teachers to set clearer assignment-specific conditions.
- Treat passing off AI-generated ideas—not only copied wording—as plagiarism.
- Use drafts and editing history as process evidence.
- AI-detector output may trigger a conversation but must not serve as sole or primary proof.
