## Generative AI Tools FAQ

- Publishing unit: Eberly Center for Teaching Excellence and Educational Innovation
- Official source: https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/technology/aitools/index.html

---

CMU’s Academic Integrity Policy already implicitly covers generative AI because it may be unauthorized assistance. The university policy intentionally allows instructors to define what is authorized or unauthorized and what constitutes plagiarism or cheating.

CMU recommends that course policies specify:

- whether and when AI tools are authorized;
- how students should cite assistance or AI-generated content and ideas; and
- why the rule supports the course’s learning objectives.

Because students take courses with different rules, instructors should not assume that students can infer the correct rule. The resource advises discussing academic integrity early, giving examples, and inviting questions before work is due.

The page warns that ChatGPT may sound convincing while being factually incorrect and cannot validate the information or sourcing it has generated.

## Example course-policy language

- Additional official source: https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/technology/aitools/academicintegrity/index.html

One model permits AI but requires the student to cite the tool’s contribution, include the specific version, and provide the exact prompt and full response in an appendix. The student remains responsible for assessing the validity and applicability of output.

The FAQ also cautions that AI detection is extremely limited and enforcement may be difficult and unreliable.

## High-school adaptation takeaways

- Define authorized and unauthorized assistance separately for each course or assignment.
- Explain the learning purpose behind restrictions rather than stating only a penalty.
- When AI is allowed, consider requiring tool/version, prompt, output, and a description of student revision.
- Students remain responsible for validating AI content; detector limitations should shape fair enforcement.
